Student Protection Manual

How to Spot Fake Jobs & Internship Scams

A comprehensive survival guide for university students and fresh graduates navigating modern online recruitment fraud.

💡

The Golden Rule of Employment

A legitimate employer or internship will ALWAYS pay you for your work. You should NEVER pay an employer to work for them. Any company asking for an application fee, training fee, security deposit, or equipment setup fee is running a scam.

01

The Upfront Training / Certificate Fee

The recruiter claims you are selected for a prestigious virtual internship (e.g., Python, AI, Web Dev). However, you must pay $50–$200 for a "mandatory training kit", "seat reservation", or "ISO certificate".

Reality: Real companies provide internal onboarding and training free of charge.
02

The Telegram / WhatsApp Interview

You are told to download Telegram or WhatsApp and message a "hiring manager". The entire interview consists of a few typed questions, followed by an immediate offer letter within 15 minutes.

Reality: Professional recruiters use corporate video platforms (Google Meet, Zoom, Teams) and formal email domains.
03

The Cashier's Check / Equipment Scam

The company mails you a digital or paper check for $3,000–$5,000 to "buy a home office MacBook and supplies". You are instructed to deposit the check and wire money to their "approved supplier".

Reality: The check is counterfeit and bounces days later. You are left responsible for the thousands wired to the scammer.
04

The Social Media / Rating Task Scam

Advertised as "Online Part-Time Student Assistant" paying $50–$100 per day. You are asked to like YouTube videos, submit 5-star Google Maps reviews, or place dummy e-commerce orders.

Reality: You will be tricked into depositing crypto or bank funds into a fake dashboard to "unlock" your earnings.
05

The Reshipping / Package Mule Scam

Hired as a "Package Inspector" or "Logistics Coordinator". You receive boxes at your home address, inspect the contents, and reship them using prepaid labels to overseas addresses.

Reality: The items were bought with stolen credit cards. You become the legal target of law enforcement investigations.
06

The Free Email Domain Impersonator

The email claims to be from a well-known multinational corporation (e.g. Amazon, Google, Microsoft), but the sender address ends with @gmail.com, @yahoo.com, or a lookalike domain.

Reality: Multi-billion dollar enterprises never use free consumer webmail for corporate recruitment.
✅

Student Pre-Application Checklist

5 checks you should perform before sharing your resume or accepting an offer.

1
Check the Official Company Careers Page: If a job opening exists, it will almost always be listed directly on company.com/careers. If it isn't there, message their official HR team.
2
Verify Recruiter Identity on LinkedIn: Search the recruiter's name and ensure their profile has genuine connections, employment history, and company endorsements.
3
Inspect the Email Domain Carefully: Beware of typosquatting (e.g. @micros0ft.com or @google-careers-portal.com instead of official corporate domains).
4
Refuse Upfront Payment Requests: Never provide credit card numbers, wire transfers, or crypto for training modules, certificates, or laptops.
5
Consult Your University Placement Cell: If an offer seems suspicious, show it to your department chair, career advisor, or college placement officer before proceeding.
📢

Where to Report Recruitment Fraud

Official agencies and platforms that investigate cyber recruitment scams.

Federal Trade Commission (FTC)

Report job scams directly to the FTC to aid national investigations against fraudulent recruiters.

ReportFraud.ftc.gov

FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3)

For wire fraud, counterfeit checks, and international identity theft scams.

ic3.gov

Job Board Support Teams

Report the suspicious listing immediately on LinkedIn, Indeed, or Handshake using their built-in 'Report Job' button.
